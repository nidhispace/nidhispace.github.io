Middle East

Mesopotamia

c. 3500–539 BCE

Often called the cradle of civilisation, Mesopotamia saw the world's first cities rise in Sumer, such as Uruk, from around 3500 BCE. Its scribes pressed wedge-shaped cuneiform into clay tablets to record trade, laws, astronomy and literature.

First cities (Uruk)Cuneiform writingCode of HammurabiEpic of GilgameshBase-60 time
Where: Between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers (modern Iraq, with parts of Syria, Turkey and Iran)
Writing: Cuneiform, from c. 3300 BCE
Government: City-states, later empires: Akkad, Babylon and Assyria
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Our 60-minute hours and 360-degree circle are widely traced to Mesopotamian base-60 mathematics. The Code of Hammurabi (c. 1754 BCE) is one of the earliest extensive written law codes.

Connections. Traded with the Indus cities, which cuneiform texts call Meluhha. Persia absorbed Babylon in 539 BCE.

Asia

Indus Valley

c. 3300–1300 BCE

At their mature peak, around 2600–1900 BCE, the Indus cities were laid out on grids with brick houses and covered drains. Their people traded far beyond the valley, and their small stamp seals carry a script that nobody can yet read.

Planned citiesCovered drainsGreat BathLothal dockyardStandard weights
Where: The Indus and Ghaggar-Hakra basins (modern Pakistan and north-west India)
Writing: Indus script, still undeciphered
Government: Unclear: no obvious palaces or royal tombs, but standard weights and bricks suggest strong planning
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Few ancient cities matched its sanitation and planning. Because the script is undeciphered, much of what we know comes from archaeology alone.

Connections. Indus seals have been found in Mesopotamia, which points to long-distance sea trade.

Africa

Ancient Egypt

c. 3100–30 BCE

Egypt grew along the Nile, whose yearly flood made farming reliable, and was unified under one king around 3100 BCE. For almost three thousand years its pharaohs built temples and pyramids while scribes kept records on papyrus.

Pyramids of GizaMummification365-day calendarPapyrusRosetta Stone
Where: The Nile Valley and Delta (modern Egypt)
Writing: Hieroglyphs from c. 3200 BCE, later hieratic and demotic
Government: Pharaonic monarchy; the king was seen as divine, supported by a large scribal bureaucracy
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Its 365-day solar calendar influenced the later Roman calendar, and surviving medical papyri show a practical approach to treatment. The Rosetta Stone, carved in 196 BCE, let Jean-François Champollion decipher hieroglyphs in 1822.

Connections. Egypt was ruled for a time by the Nubian kings of Kush (25th Dynasty), and was annexed by Rome after Cleopatra VII died in 30 BCE.

Europe

Minoans & Mycenaeans

c. 3000–1100 BCE

On Crete, the Minoans built the great palace at Knossos and a seafaring trading culture. From about 1450 BCE Mycenaean Greeks from the mainland took over Knossos, and left the earliest written Greek on Linear B tablets.

Palace of KnossosBull-leaping frescoesLinear B tabletsCitadel of Mycenae
Where: Crete and mainland Greece (Aegean Bronze Age)
Writing: Linear A (undeciphered) and Linear B (early Greek, deciphered in 1952)
Government: Palace-centred states; Mycenaean kings ruled from fortified citadels
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Greek myths of King Minos, the Minotaur and the Trojan War echo these Bronze Age worlds. Linear B, deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952, shows Greek was written more than 600 years before the alphabet.

Connections. The eruption of Thera (Santorini) around 1600 BCE buried the town of Akrotiri; the palace societies collapsed c. 1200 BCE.

Americas

Caral–Norte Chico

c. 3000–1800 BCE

Caral, in Peru's Supe Valley, flourished around 2600 BCE with large stepped platform mounds and sunken plazas, making it one of the oldest known urban centres in the Americas. Its people had no pottery and no known writing, yet built on a monumental scale.

Oldest known city in the AmericasPyramid platformsSunken circular plazasUNESCO site
Where: The Supe Valley on the north-central coast of Peru
Writing: No known writing
Government: Probably led by temple-based elites; little sign of warfare
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Caral shows that complex societies in the Americas arose at about the same time as Egypt's pyramids. It became a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.

Connections. Coastal fishing and inland cotton farming were tied together by trade; later Andean societies built on these traditions.

Africa

Kingdom of Kush

c. 2500 BCE–350 CE

South of Egypt, Kush rose around Kerma, then Napata, then Meroë. In the 8th century BCE its kings conquered Egypt and ruled as the 25th Dynasty, restoring temples and pyramid-building.

Rulers of EgyptMeroë ironworkingNubian pyramidsGold and ivory trade
Where: The Nile Valley south of Egypt (modern Sudan)
Writing: Meroitic script, readable but the language is not fully understood
Government: Sacred kings; powerful queens known as kandake
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Kush built more pyramids than Egypt, and Meroë was a major iron-working centre. Meroitic was an early African alphabetic script.

Connections. Traded gold, ivory and ebony with Egypt, later Ptolemaic and Roman Egypt.

Americas

Maya

c. 2000 BCE–900 CE

The Maya built dozens of city-states in the rainforests and lowlands of Mesoamerica, among them Tikal, Calakmul and Palenque. In the Classic period (c. 250–900 CE) their kings recorded wars, alliances and rituals on carved stone monuments.

Concept of zeroLong Count calendarAstronomyPyramid citiesCacao
Where: Southern Mexico, Guatemala, Belize and Honduras
Writing: Maya hieroglyphs, a mix of syllable and word signs, now largely deciphered
Government: Independent city-states led by divine kings
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Maya scribes used a place-value number system with a symbol for zero and tracked Venus and eclipses with great accuracy. Millions of Maya people live today and still speak Maya languages.

Connections. Trade and ideas linked the Maya with Teotihuacan in central Mexico, and they shared traditions with the earlier Olmec.

Asia

Early China

c. 1600 BCE–220 CE

Chinese civilisation grew in the Yellow River valley under the Shang, whose kings consulted their ancestors using inscribed oracle bones. The Qin unified China in 221 BCE, and the Han (206 BCE–220 CE) built a durable bureaucratic state.

Bronze castingSilkConfuciusTerracotta ArmyPaper
Where: The Yellow River and Yangtze valleys (modern China)
Writing: Oracle-bone script (Shang), ancestor of Chinese characters
Government: Dynasties: Shang, Zhou (the 'Mandate of Heaven'), Qin (unified China in 221 BCE) and Han
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Confucian thought, a standard written script and the idea of a unified empire shaped China for two thousand years. Paper was being made by the early 2nd century CE and later spread west.

Connections. The Han opened the Silk Road after Zhang Qian's missions (from c. 138 BCE), linking China to Central Asia, Persia and Rome.

Middle East

Phoenicians

c. 1200–146 BCE

The Phoenicians were merchants and sailors from city-states on the Lebanese coast who founded trading colonies from Cyprus to Spain. Their best-known colony, Carthage, was traditionally founded in 814 BCE.

Seafaring and tradePurple dyeAlphabetCarthageCedar timber
Where: The eastern Mediterranean coast (modern Lebanon), with colonies across the sea
Writing: Phoenician alphabet (c. 1050 BCE), ancestor of the Greek and Latin alphabets
Government: Independent city-states such as Tyre, Sidon and Byblos, ruled by kings and councils
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Their 22-letter alphabet, written without vowels, was adapted by the Greeks and became the ancestor of the Latin alphabet.

Connections. Carthage fought Rome in three Punic Wars before its destruction in 146 BCE.

Americas

Olmec

c. 1200–400 BCE

The Olmec built the first major civilisation of Mesoamerica around 1200 BCE. They carved enormous heads, up to about 3 metres tall, from basalt hauled many kilometres from the quarries.

Colossal stone headsRubberRitual ball gameJade carving
Where: The Gulf Coast of Mexico (Veracruz and Tabasco)
Writing: Possible early script (Cascajal Block), debated
Government: Chiefdoms centred on ceremonial cities such as San Lorenzo and La Venta
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Many later Mesoamerican traditions, including the ball game and rubber use, appear first or most clearly in Olmec contexts; scholars debate how far they influenced neighbouring peoples.

Connections. Sometimes called the 'mother culture' of Mesoamerica, although other regions developed in parallel.

Europe

Ancient Greece

c. 800–30 BCE

Classical Greece was never one state but hundreds of rival city-states that shared a language and religion. In Athens, around 508 BCE, reforms by Cleisthenes began the experiment in democracy, and thinkers like Socrates, Plato and Aristotle laid foundations for Western philosophy.

Democracy in AthensPhilosophyOlympic GamesTheatreEuclid & Archimedes
Where: The Greek peninsula, Aegean islands and colonies around the Mediterranean
Writing: Greek alphabet, adapted from Phoenician and the first with vowels
Government: Independent city-states (poleis): Athens developed democracy, Sparta an oligarchy
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Greek ideas about democracy, logic, geometry and drama still shape modern education. After Alexander the Great (d. 323 BCE), Greek culture spread across Egypt and Asia in the Hellenistic world.

Connections. Alexander's armies reached the Indus in 326 BCE, where Greek and Indian traditions met, as in the Gandhara art.

Europe

Ancient Rome

753 BCE–476 CE

Rome grew from a small city on the Tiber into the ruler of the Mediterranean world. After a republic governed by elected magistrates and the Senate, Augustus became its first emperor in 27 BCE.

Roads & aqueductsRoman lawConcrete architectureLatinLegions
Where: Italy at first, then the whole Mediterranean world
Writing: Latin alphabet
Government: Monarchy, then Republic (509 BCE), then Empire under Augustus (27 BCE)
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Roman law, Latin (the parent of the Romance languages) and engineering such as roads, aqueducts and concrete domes like the Pantheon still define much of the modern world. The Western Empire ended in 476 CE; the Eastern (Byzantine) Empire lasted until 1453.

Connections. Roman merchants traded with India for pepper and gold coins found in southern India show that link; Rome annexed Egypt in 30 BCE.

Middle East

Achaemenid Persia

550–330 BCE

Founded by Cyrus the Great in 550 BCE, the Achaemenid Empire grew to span the Balkans, Egypt and the Indus, the largest the world had yet seen. It was run through satraps, a network of roads and a shared administration in Aramaic.

Largest empire of its dayRoyal RoadPersepolisZoroastrianism
Where: From Iran to the Indus, Egypt and the Balkans at its peak
Writing: Old Persian cuneiform, with Elamite and Aramaic for administration
Government: 'King of Kings' ruling through provincial governors (satraps)
Legacy & connections

Legacy. Cyrus's capture of Babylon in 539 BCE let exiled peoples return home; the Cyrus Cylinder is sometimes cited as an early statement of tolerance, though historians debate that framing.

Connections. Fought the Greeks in the Persian Wars (490–479 BCE) and fell to Alexander the Great, who burned Persepolis in 330 BCE.
